The Importance of Wildlife Corridors This Summer

December 2025  ·  5 min read

Summer in Australia follows a familiar script: climbing temperatures, long dry spells, restless heatwaves, and a rising bushfire threat. For our native wildlife, these conditions can be brutal. When habitat is broken into isolated pockets, animals lose the freedom to move, hide, cool down, and recover.

Wildlife corridors change that. A connected landscape is not just good ecology. It is life support. Corridors help animals escape danger, find food and water, and bounce back after fire or extreme weather. And as summers get hotter, the value of these living pathways rises fast.

Summer pressure

Why Wildlife Corridors matter more in Summer

Corridors give wildlife options when conditions turn extreme

During heatwaves, animals move instinctively toward shade, water, and cooler pockets in the landscape. In heavily cleared or urbanised areas, those options often vanish.

Continuous habitat, even at a small scale, opens safe pathways that let:

  • Birds move between feeding and watering sites
  • Lizards and small mammals slip into shaded understorey
  • Pollinators track flowering resources
  • Nocturnal species cross open areas without overheating

Without these routes, animals can become trapped in baking habitat. Heat stress, dehydration, and reduced survival quickly follow.

Corridors improve survival during bushfires

Research from CSIRO and the Bushfire and Natural Hazards CRC shows that wildlife corridors:

  • Offer escape routes during fire
  • Shelter animals in cooler, damp refuges
  • Help species return and rebuild after the flames pass

Fragmented habitat leaves wildlife cornered with no safe exits. Connected habitat lets animals move around fire fronts and return once conditions settle. Even small plantings along streets or back fences add up, improving the odds of survival when fire strikes.

Corridors keep food and water within reach

Summer squeezes Australia’s natural food supply. Nectar dries up, insects retreat, seeds drop early, and freshwater shrinks.

Corridors create a chain of microhabitats where wildlife can move between:

  • Flowering shrubs
  • Shaded understorey
  • Water sources
  • Hollow-bearing trees

This mobility is often the difference between making it through the season or not.

Start at home

Wildlife Corridors Begin at Home

Large reserves matter, but most biodiversity loss happens on private land. That means every backyard, balcony, courtyard, or verge can play a role in stitching fragmented landscapes back together.

Small habitat patches connect into bigger ones. And those networks keep wildlife moving.

What you can do in your own yard

Shrubs and woody bushes

  • Create shade and shelter
  • Support small birds, skinks, insects, pollinators
  • Offer privacy and noise buffering

Trees, both living and dead

  • Living trees give food, cover, and nesting space
  • Safe standing dead trees provide hollows for birds and marsupials
  • Fallen logs feed fungi and boost soil nutrients

Leaf piles and compost

Skip the green bin. Instead:

  • Keep contained compost piles away from fire-prone edges
  • Encourage the insects and moisture that form microhabitats
  • Add veg scraps and eggshells to build soil health
  • Healthy soil leads to stronger plants and better habitat

Wildflowers and native groundcovers

  • Low maintenance
  • Nectar for pollinators
  • A base layer that supports the whole food web

Choose the right plants

Use tools like the GroNATIVE app, local nurseries, or SEEK / Google Lens to identify local species. Think about the wildlife you love most and plant for them.

Add water features

  • Shallow ponds or dishes
  • Stones or marbles for safe insect landings
  • Slow-moving water to reduce mosquito risk
Without a yard

If You Don’t Have a Yard

You can still help connect the landscape.

On a balcony

  • Native grasses in pots
  • Flowering shrubs in containers
  • Water dishes for birds and insects
  • Window decals to prevent bird strike
  • Bee bricks for solitary native bees

In the community

  • Join a community garden
  • Advocate for native vegetation in local parks
  • Encourage native plantings in apartment courtyards
  • Support wildlife-friendly verge projects

Each small patch strengthens the bigger network. And that network keeps wildlife breathing, moving, and surviving Australia’s toughest months.

Fire safety

How Wildlife Corridors Support Fire Safety

Well-designed corridors do more than protect wildlife. They help create safer, more resilient landscapes by:

  • Slowing wind, which can reduce fire spread
  • Giving animals safer escape routes
  • Breaking the landscape into moist, diverse patches that are harder for fire to sweep through
  • Helping ecosystems recover faster after a burn

At the landscape scale, we are already showing what successful wildlife corridors look like through projects such as the Bandicoot Superhighway in South Australia. Delivered in partnership with the Hills and Fleurieu Landscape Board, the Sturt Upper Reaches Landcare Group, the Nature Conservation Society of South Australia and the Australian Government, this community-led effort is reconnecting fragmented habitat for the endangered Southern Brown Bandicoot by creating a safe movement route through the Mount Lofty Ranges.  

In just two years, the Bandicoot Superhighway delivered conservation outcomes across 216 hectares of priority habitat, restored and planted 34,699 native seedlings, and brought communities together through workshops, planting days and presentations attended by more than 900 people, including 215 hands-on volunteers.

The project also strengthened scientific knowledge. More than 110 bandicoot sightings were uploaded to a new citizen-science portal, supported by over 800 camera-trapping days across multiple properties. These records confirm how corridors help bandicoots move, forage and survive in increasingly fragmented landscapes.

The benefits reached well beyond ecology. The project invested over $211,000 into local biodiversity work and is projected to generate $1.3 million in economic activity within five years, showing how wildlife corridors support both nature and regional communities.

Working with landholders, partner organisations, community groups and government, we are demonstrating how restoring habitat connections reduces extinction risk, strengthens ecosystems and helps native wildlife move safely across the landscape. These connections matter even more during high-risk summer seasons when linked refuge zones can mean survival.

Connection

A Connected Landscape Is a Resilient Landscape

Wildlife corridors boost biodiversity, support water and soil health, improve fire resilience, and give native species a real chance in a warming world.

This summer and every summer ahead, the most powerful action we can take is simple: connect the habitat around us. Stitch together yards, balconies, verges, parks, and bushland. Build pathways. Build refuge.

Build a landscape where wildlife has room to move and room to survive.
