Genetic Code of Koalas

Port Macquarie and north-east New South Wales   ·   Funded 2013

You cannot protect a population you cannot tell apart from the next one. We funded the Australian Museum to read the DNA of koalas on the New South Wales mid-north coast and find out whether they were still reaching each other.

26,558Koala genes identifiedUp from the 15,500 found in earlier work, including immune and detoxification genes described for the first time.
54Scientists involvedAcross 29 research institutions in seven countries, over five years of work.

Genome figures reported by the Koala Genome Consortium in 2018, published in Nature Genetics. The Foundation for National Parks & Wildlife funded the mid-north coast population genetics work in 2013.

The problem

A koala can be surrounded by trees and still be stranded

Habitat loss is usually described as forest disappearing, and that is only half of it. The other half is what happens to the forest left behind. Roads, clearing and development cut it into fragments, and koalas that once moved freely between them stop meeting, stop breeding with each other, and slowly become separate small populations rather than one large one.

Small isolated populations lose genetic diversity, and diversity is what lets a species absorb a shock. It is the difference between a disease outbreak thinning a population and ending it. The trouble is that none of this is visible from the ground. A survey can count koalas in two reserves and tell you nothing at all about whether those koalas are still connected.

The approach

Reading connection out of the DNA itself

In 2013 the Foundation for National Parks & Wildlife funded the Australian Museum to answer that question for koalas around Port Macquarie and the wider mid-north coast. Koalas in Queensland, New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory had been listed as vulnerable under national environmental law the year before, and whether the remaining populations were still connected had become a live question.

The method does not require following an animal anywhere. Microsatellite markers are short repeating sequences that change quickly between generations, so they show whether animals in neighbouring patches are currently breeding with one another. Mitochondrial DNA passes down the maternal line almost unchanged, so it carries the deeper history of where a population came from. Read together, the two tell you how connected these koalas are now and how connected they used to be.

The finding

The diversity is still there, and the corridors are what hold it

The result was better than it might have been. Both the microsatellite and the mitochondrial data showed that koalas around Port Macquarie still carry relatively high genetic diversity, and that gene flow between groups has not collapsed. This is a population with something worth protecting rather than one already in genetic trouble.

The conclusion that matters for anyone managing that landscape follows directly. Because the connectivity is still intact, the dispersal corridors that maintain it have to stay open. Diversity of this kind is far easier to keep than to rebuild, and the moment the corridors close, the clock starts running the other way.

The wider work

The same year, in the same building, something larger started

In 2013 a small group of Australian scientists met to pool what they knew about koala populations, genetics and disease. They had a second motive alongside the koala. No mammal genome had ever been sequenced and assembled from scratch by a solely Australian-led group, and they wanted to change that. The Koala Genome Consortium came out of that meeting, and five years later it had grown to 54 scientists across 29 institutions in seven countries.

The Consortium sequenced three animals, one of them using long-read technology that produces very long uninterrupted stretches of sequence. The result, published in Nature Genetics in 2018, was the best-quality marsupial genome produced to that point and comparable in quality to the human genome, which had taken thirteen years and three billion US dollars. Among the stakeholders who provided access to live animals was the Koala Hospital at Port Macquarie, on the same stretch of coast as the population work we had funded.

A genome is a reference, not an answer

What makes the koala genome useful is how specific it lets other questions become. The Consortium found that koalas carry two large expansions in the Cytochrome P450 family, the genes that break down toxins. That is what lets an animal live on eucalypt leaves that would kill most mammals, and it also explains why koalas metabolise anti-inflammatory pain relief and some antibiotics unusually, which changes how a vet treats an injured one.

  • 24 bitter taste genes more than any other Australian marsupial
  • A duplicated aquaporin gene the ability to taste water in a leaf
  • Koala-specific milk proteins active against the chlamydia strain that blinds them
  • Immune genes mapped the genetic basis of vaccine response
  • Retrovirus origins why some animals are more susceptible than others

The taste genes explain the choosiness that makes koalas so vulnerable to habitat loss, since they feed on roughly twenty of more than six hundred eucalypt species and need leaves that are more than half water. The milk proteins were the surprise. Koala young spend around six months in the pouch without a working immune system, and the milk that sustains them turns out to carry proteins active against bacteria and fungi, including the strain of Chlamydia pecorum that causes blindness and infertility in adults.

The long view

The genome also holds a record of every crash koalas have already survived

Genome sequence can be used to reconstruct how a population rose and fell over thousands of generations, and the koala's record is sobering. The Consortium reports a peak of somewhere between 50,000 and 86,000 animals more than 100,000 years ago, then a rapid decline 30,000 to 40,000 years ago that left the population at about a tenth of its former size. That collapse lines up with the wider extinction of Australia's megafauna, which points to a substantial change in climate or habitat.

Numbers then held at that reduced level until European settlement, when millions of koalas were shot for their skins, wiping out populations entirely in some areas and leaving severe genetic bottlenecks across southern Australia. What we are conserving today is the remainder of that.

Set against that history, the mid-north coast result reads differently. A population that still holds diversity and connectivity, after everything the species has already been through, is not a minor finding. It is one of the better hands left to play, which is exactly why the Consortium's own conclusion is that genetic monitoring has to run alongside habitat protection rather than instead of it.

What happens next

Why a 2013 result still matters

A great deal has changed for koalas since this work was funded. The 2019 and 2020 bushfires burned through a large part of their range on the New South Wales coast. As of 2026 the koala is no longer listed as vulnerable in the eastern states: the listing was raised to endangered on 12 February 2022, and it covers Queensland, New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory. Some populations are projected to decline by up to half over the next three generations, which is roughly twenty years.

That makes the baseline more valuable rather than less. Knowing what the genetic diversity and connectivity of these koalas looked like before the fires is the only way to measure what the fires cost, and the only way to judge whether the corridors being replanted now are doing the job they were meant to do. Genetics is slow science, and it repays patience by making later answers possible.

It also points the funding somewhere specific. If the corridors are what hold the diversity, then the tree planting, the weed control and the habitat linkage work we fund elsewhere are not separate from this research. They are what the research told us to do.
