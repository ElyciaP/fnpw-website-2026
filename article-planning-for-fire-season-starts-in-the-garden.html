Planning for Fire Season Starts in the Garden

November 2025

As the Australia summer heat starts to build, so does the risk of bushfire, not just in remote forested areas, but around homes, properties and in our wider communities. At the same time, this is a powerful moment for community togetherness, land healing and supporting our native landscapes to recover and thrive. We can work hand-in-hand: preparing our homes, supporting our neighbours, restoring our land.

In this blog we explore:

  • Why native plants matter in bushfire prevention and land-healing
  • What you can do at home or as a neighbourhood to make your place safer
  • How the Fire Wise Project and the Fire Wise Learning Hub offer resources and tools for action
The science

Why Native Plants & Smart Vegetation Matter

It’s tempting to think that bare ground or removing all vegetation is the safest route when fires loom. But fire science tells us a more nuanced story. Planting native vegetation with the right spacing and plant-types can actually help slow fires and save lives.

Here are a few key scientific points:

  • Fires move fastest through fine dry fuels like grasses and exposed ground litter, especially when driven by wind.
  • A moderately dense canopy of native trees/shrubs can provide shade, maintain moist soil, reduce local wind speeds and offer a barrier to ember attack and radiant heat.
  • After a bushfire, the ecosystem goes through stages of recovery: first colonised by short-lived species that protect soil and provide shelter, then gradually more mature vegetation returns.
  • Native vegetation isn’t inherently a fire hazard; in fact, with the right design and management, it supports both biodiversity and resilience.

So when we think about preparing for summer and restoring our land, the message is: don’t just clear everything, think about strategic planting, nurturing native plants, and working as a community.

At home

What You Can Do at Home

Here’s a checklist of practical steps you (and your neighbours/community) can take. Adapt them to your context, climate and local native plant list.

Secure your property and create defensible space

  • Remove or regularly clear fine fuels: dry leaves, grass clippings, bark mulch near buildings.
  • Keep shrubs and trees spaced so fire doesn’t readily climb from ground to canopy.
  • Prune lower branches of trees to reduce ladder fuels.
  • Ensure your house/garden access is clear for fire-engine access and evacuation if required.
  • Make sure your emergency plan is up to date, and your household knows it.

Use native plants (and the right ones!)

  • Choose native plants with lower flammability and plant them in the right locations. The Fire Wise project emphasises the strategic use of native vegetation rather than wholesale clearing.
  • Use design logic: e.g., waterwise, shade-keeping, windbreaks.
  • Maintain your plantings: keep them healthy, irrigated (where appropriate), remove dead material.
  • Consider ground-covers that hold moisture and reduce fine fuels.

Community and neighbourhood involvement

  • Talk with your neighbours: a bushfire-safe community is a connected, informed one. Share ideas, join planting days, form local groups.
  • Host or attend community native-planting days: these build local ecosystem resilience and social cohesion.
  • Visit, support or volunteer at local seed banks (native seeds) or community nurseries to grow local plants for fire-wise landscaping.
  • Share resources and knowledge from the Learning Hub (see below) so everyone is on the same page.

After a fire or during recovery

  • Monitor the site: photograph and revisit. Drops in vegetation or structure need attention.
  • Control invasive species quickly – they can outcompete native seedlings and create new fuel loads.
  • Re-plant missing or keystone native species if natural regeneration isn’t sufficient.
  • Consider cultural burning or Indigenous-led practices if your local context allows, honouring Country and traditional knowledge.
Working together

The Science + The Community: How They Connect

At its heart, fire-prevention and land-healing is both science (fire behaviour, ecology, vegetation management) and community care (neighbours, planting, shared land, Indigenous knowledge). Bridging these gives us real resilience.

For instance: the Fire Wise theory highlights three key fire risks: radiant heat, wind speed and ember attack. By planting the right natives, spaced and maintained, communities can reduce those risks: shade keeps ground moist, trees deflect wind/embers, and healthy vegetation slows flame spread. It’s a simple concept, but when executed across many properties in a neighbourhood, the effect multiplies.

And when a community plants together, shares seeds and knowledge, the social fabric becomes stronger. People know each other, share responsibilities, look out for each other, and that’s another layer of fire safety.

Go deeper

Resources & Links: Dive Deeper

Here are some top resources to help you, your neighbourhood or your organisation get started:

  • Fire Wise Learning Hub – Explore their learning resources, project case-studies and plant lists. Visit: https://firewisehub.online/
  • Fire Wise Resources page – https://firewisehub.online/resources/
  • FNPW Fire Wise Project – https://fnpw.org.au/project/fire-wise/ – gives project overview, science-background, how you can get involved.
  • Helping the Bush Recover (by Trust for Nature) – Good reading on ecological recovery, timing and weed control. trustfornature.org.au
  • Practical plant lists & fire-wise landscaping reports (e.g., The Crossing Land’s NSW report) thecrossingland.org.au

Tip: Bookmark those URLs, share them in your community newsletter or local social-media group, perhaps even organise a “bushfire-safe native planting” morning.

What's at stake

Why It Matters And What’s At Stake

When we don’t act, the stakes are high: the 2019-20 “Black Summer” fires illustrated how ecosystems, homes and communities can be devastated. Recovery takes years, biodiversity suffers, and our native species and cultural heritage are under threat.

But when we do act:

  • Homes become safer
  • Landscapes become more resilient
  • Biodiversity is supported
  • Communities become stronger, connected, proactive

And importantly: embracing native species and thoughtful vegetation means we’re healing our land, not just defending it.

This summer, let’s come together: behind our fences, in our streets, in our neighbourhoods. Let’s plant with purpose, look after our native landscapes, and support each other. Fire preparedness it’s about smart design, native planting, shared knowledge and community care.
