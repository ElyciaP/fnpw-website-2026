Healing the Land

We restore and rehabilitate landscapes affected by bushfires, floods, and human activities like unsustainable land clearing, through our Landscape Resilience Program.

1.2MPlants in the ground

Trees, shrubs and seedlings planted across priority restoration sites. FNPW Impact Report.

28Projects funded

Revegetation, wetland repair, bushfire recovery and cultural land management.

7States and territories

Across the eastern seaboard, the Top End and the west.

Why it matters

What this work does

Much of Australia is damaged rather than destroyed, and damaged land can heal. These projects replant habitat, restore wetlands and waterways, recover country after bushfire and support First Nations cultural land management.

Healing the land is slow, patient work measured in seasons and seedlings. It is also the work that turns a map of loss back into habitat.

How we do it

  • We fund revegetation, restoration and bushfire recovery at scale
  • We support cultural fire and caring-for-Country programs led by First Nations communities
  • We stay for the follow-up: watering, weeding, monitoring, replanting
From seed to habitat

Where your support goes.

Healing the land takes more than putting a tree in the ground. Only about a tenth of Australia’s native plants are available as seed, so we built a network to grow the rest, and your support carries each plant through every stage.

  1. 01

    Collecting seed

    Volunteers collect seed from local ecosystems, so what goes back into the ground belongs there.

  2. 02

    Growing it on

    More than thirty community-led nurseries grow the seed into locally adapted plants and bank what is rare or threatened. Some species take one to two years to be ready.

  3. 03

    Planting

    The plants go back into degraded land in national parks and on public and private land, with First Nations partners, Landcare groups and landholders.

  4. 04

    Caring and measuring

    We stay for the follow-up of watering, weeding, monitoring and replanting, and report what survives and grows.

Featured project

Yarrahapinni Wetlands

Floodgates and levee walls removed, tidal flows returned: a wetland on the Macleay coming back to life after decades of damage.

It shows what healing at scale looks like when you undo the original damage rather than work around it, and then stay long enough to watch the system come back on its own terms.
